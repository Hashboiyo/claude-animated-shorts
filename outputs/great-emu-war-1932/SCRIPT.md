# Great Emu War — sourced narration

## Facts and sources

- The 1932 cull responded to crop and fence damage on Western Australian soldier-settler farms; emus scattered, the operation failed to solve the problem, and further military requests in 1934, 1943 and 1948 were refused. [National Archives of Australia, “Environmental issues from the not so distant past”](https://www.naa.gov.au/blog/environmental-issues-not-so-distant-past).
- Three Royal Australian Artillery soldiers arrived at Campion on 2 November. A 4 November ambush of roughly 1,000 birds ended with a gun jam and 12 birds killed. Military intervention resumed later in November and ended on 10 December. [ABC Midwest & Wheatbelt, 10 December 2022](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2022-12-10/great-emu-war-90-years-on-army-wheatbelt-battle-history/101752238).
- The force brought two Lewis guns and 10,000 rounds. A truck-mounted gun failed; the truck could not gain on the birds. A second intervention followed the first withdrawal. [Australian Geographic, 18 October 2016](https://www.australiangeographic.com.au/nature-wildlife/2016/10/australias-emu-wars/).
- Rough terrain made truck-mounted fire ineffective; the soldiers suffered no human casualties. [Forces News, 5 July 2016](https://www.forcesnews.com/services/tri-service/great-emu-war-how-australia-was-beaten-bird).
- Meredith's reported renewed-operation figures were 986 confirmed kills from 9,860 rounds. These are claims in his report, not independently verified totals; sources disagree on casualties from the first phase. [War History Online](https://www.warhistoryonline.com/featured/emu-war-australia.html), cross-checked with [MeatEater](https://www.themeateater.com/conservation/wildlife-management/the-great-emu-war-of-1932).

## Accuracy and staging guardrails

- The exact requested hook uses “lost” as comic shorthand for a failed pest-control operation. It was not a declared conventional war and there was no emu surrender treaty, organised emu command, or bird counterattack. The narration qualifies the outcome near the end.
- Do not depict emus as bulletproof or as literally outrunning bullets; show scattering before guns can follow.
- The confetti-and-fan line is an explicitly signposted analogy, not a historical claim. The “couldn't fly” callback refers to emus being flightless.
- Do not depict graphic animal injury. The jam and truck gags can play through scattering, dust and soldiers' reactions.
- No invented dialogue or historical quotations. No captions or labels.

## Narration

Australia once sent soldiers with machine guns to fight birds… and lost.

It was nineteen thirty-two. In Western Australia, migrating emus were wrecking wheat crops and fences. Struggling farmers, including First World War veterans, asked the government for help.

The answer arrived at Campion: three soldiers, two Lewis machine guns, and ten thousand rounds of ammunition.

Against birds that couldn't even fly.

On November fourth, the troops waited beside a dam. About a thousand emus approached. Perfect. The gun jammed. Only twelve birds were killed before the rest escaped.

So they mounted a machine gun on a truck. Unfortunately, the ride was too bumpy to aim, and the truck couldn't catch the birds.

Luckily, the soldiers suffered no casualties. Unluckily, the wheat still had a problem.

You see, the emus scattered into small groups. Think of trying to sweep up confetti while someone keeps switching on the fan. More firepower didn't solve the moving-target problem.

The army withdrew, returned later in November, and withdrew again in December. In the renewed campaign, Major Meredith reported nine hundred eighty-six birds killed with nine thousand eight hundred sixty rounds.

The birds weren't bulletproof. The operation simply failed to solve the farmers' problem. Later requests for military help were refused.

The emus couldn't fly. Apparently, they didn't need to.
